# Nutrition Rebuild + Apple Health/Watch Integration — Audit & Plan

Prepared for handoff to Claude Code. Covers: (1) a code/design audit of the current app with an eye to technical debt, (2) the target architecture for an in-house meal/food logging system to replace MyFitnessPal-total-entry, and (3) the Apple Health + Watch integration ask. Section 6 is the actual punch list to hand Claude Code; everything above it is the reasoning behind it.

## 1. Current state, in one paragraph

Nutrition today is a single `nutrition_logs` row per day: `calories`, `protein_g`, `carbs_g`, `fat_g`, `source` (`manual` or `healthkit`). There is no concept of a meal, a food, a quantity, or a recipe anywhere in the schema or the app. `NutritionEntryView` is a form where you type four numbers for a whole day (originally meant to mirror pasting in MFP's daily totals) plus a trend chart. `HealthKitManager.fetchDailyNutrition` reads Apple Health's aggregate dietary totals (which MFP writes to Health) and `HealthSyncService` upserts them as `source: healthkit`, but only into days that aren't already manually logged. This whole nutrition subsystem is a placeholder that was explicitly designed around "paste in MFP's daily total" — it was never meant to be the real system, and it shows.

Workouts, by contrast, are relatively mature: routines, sets, drop sets, supersets, cardio sessions, an adherence score, and — notably — a working local-first offline queue (`OfflineWorkoutQueue`, SwiftData-backed, syncs on reconnect). There is no HealthKit *write* path anywhere (the app never creates an `HKWorkout`), no Watch target in the Xcode project at all, and no use of `WorkoutKit`/`HKWorkoutSession`/`WatchConnectivity` anywhere in the codebase — confirmed by grep, zero hits. Apple Health is currently read-only, and only for steps, sleep, and the dietary aggregate types.

## 2. Audit: flaws and technical debt worth fixing regardless of the new features

Ranked roughly by how much they'll bite you later.

**2.1 — `nutrition_logs` is a daily aggregate, not a log.** This is the big one and it's not really a "bug" — it's the wrong shape for where the app is going. A day-granularity row can't represent "what did I eat at lunch," can't support search/barcode/recipes, and can't be edited per-item (today, fixing one wrong number means re-entering the whole day). Everything in your ask (per-meal entry, saved meals, recipes, repeat-day) requires a real line-item schema. This isn't a patch, it's a new subsystem alongside the old one during migration — see Section 4.

**2.2 — The HealthKit nutrition sync actively fights the direction you want to go.** `HealthSyncService` currently pulls `dietaryEnergyConsumed`/protein/carbs/fat from Apple Health (i.e., from MFP, since MFP is what's writing to Health) and silently backfills any day you haven't manually logged. Once you have an in-house food log, this sync becomes actively harmful unless it's gated — it'll happily overwrite a day you logged in-house-but-forgot-to-\"finish\" with a stale MFP number, or worse, coexist confusingly with real per-meal data. This needs to become the "switch" you described: a `nutrition_source` user preference (`in_house` vs `healthkit_manual`) that this sync path checks and no-ops against when the switch is on `in_house`.

**2.3 — No workout data ever reaches Apple Health.** Every set, cardio session, and workout you log stays inside Supabase; HealthKit is read-only for you. That's a missed opportunity independent of the Watch ask — writing an `HKWorkout` (with route/energy for cardio types) on `finishWorkout`/cardio-session-end means your training data shows up in Apple's own Activity rings, Trends, and any other app you use that reads Health, for very little extra work (`HKWorkoutBuilder`). Worth doing even before touching the Watch.

**2.4 — There's no Watch app, and nothing in the entitlements or project file suggests one was ever scoped.** `FitnessTracker.entitlements` has exactly one capability (`com.apple.developer.healthkit`). A Watch companion target needs its own bundle id, its own entitlements (HealthKit + possibly background modes for workout sessions), and — this matters for scoping — real device testing, since workout session APIs behave differently in the Watch Simulator than on a real wrist. Flag this now: this is the single most expensive item in your whole ask, bigger than the entire food-logging rebuild in engineering time, mainly because of the new-target setup and on-device test loop, not the API surface itself.

**2.5 — Minor debt, fix opportunistically:**
- `NutritionLog.date` / `nutrition_logs.date` round-trips through a `"yyyy-MM-dd"` string end to end instead of a typed `Date`, matched by `DateFormatting.date(fromISODate:)` scattered across call sites. Fine as-is (it's consistent with how `step_logs`/`sleep_logs` do it too), but worth keeping in mind if the new food-log tables introduce timestamps (meal `logged_at`) alongside a `date` column — don't let two different "what day is this" representations disagree.
- `NutritionRepository.loadAllLogs()` fakes "all time" with `fetchRecent(days: 3650)` (a row-count limit function called with a huge number). Harmless today at daily granularity, but if the same pattern gets reused for a per-meal-entry table it stops being harmless — thousands of rows/day-equivalent. Give the new tables real range/pagination queries from the start rather than inheriting this shortcut.
- `UserGoal` has no fiber field, and neither does `nutrition_logs`. Needs a migration either way.
- `CardioSessionMonitor` is a thin 29-line in-memory pointer to "the active session," not a real session/HealthKit integration — fine for today's manual start/log flow, but it's the piece that will need to grow (or be replaced) if cardio sessions become HealthKit-backed per 2.3.
- Migrations are hand-pasted into the Supabase SQL editor (no working local CLI, per your own notes) — worth being extra careful with the food-log migration set below since it's the largest single batch of new tables the project has added at once; consider getting the CLI working before this batch rather than after, so a mistake is a local rollback instead of a manual SQL fix in production.

None of 2.5 blocks anything — they're cleanup, not redesign.

## 3. Target architecture: in-house food & meal logging

**Design precedent already in this codebase:** `exercises` (global catalog + `is_custom` flag for user-added ones, looked up by `ExerciseRepository`, searched/picked via `ExercisePickerView`). This is exactly the right shape for `foods`, and it means the search UX pattern (search a shared catalog, fall back to "add your own") doesn't need to be invented — it needs to be copied.

**Core entities** (`supabase/migrations/0031...` onward):

- **`foods`** — the catalog. `name`, `brand` (nullable), `serving_size`, `serving_unit`, per-serving `calories`, `protein_g`, `carbs_g`, `fat_g`, `fiber_g`, `barcode` (nullable, unique when present), `source` (`seed` / `off` [Open Food Facts] / `user`), `created_by` (nullable, for user-added foods), `is_verified`. Row-level security: readable by everyone, but a food a user adds is theirs to edit until/unless you build a shared-catalog moderation flow later (start simple: every user's custom foods are private to them, exactly like custom exercises today).
- **`meal_slots`** — the user's configured meals-per-day, e.g. Preworkout/Breakfast/Lunch/Dinner/Snacks, each with a `sort_order` and `name`; user-editable count and names (per-user table, not a hardcoded enum) so "1 to X meals a day" is actually configurable rather than a fixed list.
- **`meal_entries`** — one row per food logged into a meal on a day: `user_id`, `date`, `meal_slot_id`, `food_id`, `quantity` (multiplier on the food's serving), `logged_at`. This is the real per-meal log; `nutrition_logs`'s daily totals become a *derived view* over this table (sum by day) rather than a separately-maintained row — see 3.4.
- **`recipes`** + **`recipe_ingredients`** — a recipe is a named, saveable food composed of other foods with quantities; a recipe can itself be logged into a meal like a single food (its totals are the sum of its ingredients, computed once and cached on the recipe row, recomputed on edit).
- **`saved_meals`** + **`saved_meal_items`** — a snapshot of a meal_slot's contents (e.g. "my usual breakfast") that can be re-applied to any day/slot in one action.
- **Ad-hoc day repeat** — "repeat yesterday" or "repeat meals from day X" doesn't need its own table: it's a bulk-insert of new `meal_entries` rows copying another date's rows onto the target date. This is for the "just replay what I actually ate on some specific past date" case and stays a straight copy, not a pointer to anything that could later change.
- **`saved_days`** + **`saved_day_items`** (revision from the original "repeat day" note below — Liam wants to explicitly curate and name good days, not only replay arbitrary past dates). A saved day is a *named*, deliberately-kept snapshot across every meal slot at once — "Perfect Cut Day," "Leg Day Fuel," whatever he calls it — captured with one "Save this day" action from the day view, which snapshots that date's `meal_entries` (food/recipe + quantity, per slot) into `saved_day_items` under a new `saved_days` row. Repeating one applies its items to any target date in one action, the same mechanism as the ad-hoc repeat above. Deliberately a snapshot, not a live reference to `saved_meals` per slot — editing a saved meal later shouldn't retroactively change a saved day that happens to reuse the same food, and a saved day doesn't require every meal in it to also exist as an independently-saved meal. A browse/picker view should show each saved day's totals (calories/protein/carbs/fat/fiber) at a glance, since the whole point is picking one that fits — this is also the natural place to surface "these saved days fit your remaining budget today" once Section 7's Direction A (live remaining-budget view) exists, without needing Direction A's actual solver.

**3.1 — Fiber.** Add `fiber_g` to `foods` (per-serving) and add a `fiber_g_target` column to `user_goals` alongside the existing macro targets, so it shows up in `MyGoalsView`/`AdjustNutritionTargetsView`/`EditPhaseView` the same way protein/carbs/fat targets already do. Daily fiber total is then just another sum over `meal_entries` joined to `foods`, same as calories/protein/carbs/fat.

**3.2 — Search.** Full-text search (`pg_trgm` or Postgres `tsvector`) over `foods.name`/`brand`, scoped first to the user's own previously-logged foods (fastest, most relevant — "what did I eat last time I typed 'chicken'") then the shared catalog. When nothing matches, fall back to an external food database.

**3.3 — Barcode scanning.** `AVFoundation`'s built-in barcode detection (`AVCaptureMetadataOutput`, no extra dependency) reads the UPC/EAN; look it up first in `foods.barcode` (cache-hit path, instant), then against **Open Food Facts** (free, no API key, no rate-limit headaches, decent packaged-food coverage — better fit than USDA FoodData Central, which is stronger on generic/raw ingredients than UK/Irish packaged products) if the local catalog misses. A hit from Open Food Facts gets inserted into `foods` with `source: 'off'` so the next scan of the same product is a cache hit. This mirrors MFP's own crowd-sourced-growth model without needing to build or license a food database from scratch.

**3.4 — The switch.** Add `nutrition_source` to `user_preferences` (`in_house` / `healthkit_manual`, default `in_house` once this ships). When `in_house`: `NutritionEntryView` shows the meal-based UI (Section 5's UI sketch), `nutrition_logs`-style daily totals are computed live from `meal_entries`, and `HealthSyncService.performSync` skips the nutrition portion entirely (fixes 2.2). When `healthkit_manual`: today's behavior is preserved unchanged — this is the rollback path if the new system isn't working for you yet, and it's also just correct: `nutrition_logs` as a table doesn't go away, it becomes the fallback/legacy path rather than getting deleted.

**3.5 — Offline entry.** `OfflineWorkoutQueue` is the pattern to reuse, not reinvent: local-first SwiftData write, client-generated id shared between local and remote rows, background flush on reconnect via `NetworkMonitor`. A `meal_entries` insert while offline (e.g. logging lunch with no signal) should queue the same way a set does today.

## 4. Migration path from the old system

Don't delete `nutrition_logs` or its repository. Once `meal_entries` exists, backfill is impossible for old rows (there's no way to reconstruct "what foods made up that 2,300 kcal day" from a single aggregate number), so historical trend data before the switch-over date has to keep reading from `nutrition_logs`, while data after it reads from the `meal_entries` sum. The cleanest approach: `NutritionRepository` grows a method that reads from whichever source has data for a given date, preferring `meal_entries` when present — the trend chart in `NutritionEntryView` then just keeps working across the cutover date without a visible seam.

## 5. Apple Health / Apple Watch integration

Your ask has two genuinely different pieces — worth splitting because they differ hugely in cost:

**5.1 — Import workouts Apple Health/Watch already recorded, into our DB.** This is the cheap, high-value half. It needs zero new Xcode targets: `HealthKitManager` already reads from HealthKit, it just needs to also request `.workoutType()` and query `HKWorkoutQuery`/`HKSampleQueryDescriptor<HKWorkout>`, then map each into a `cardio_sessions` (or a new `imported_workouts`) row — duration, type (map `HKWorkoutActivityType` to your existing `CardioType` enum where it fits), energy, distance, and average heart rate if present. Any incline treadmill walk you already do from the Watch's native Workout app today would show up in our app automatically once this sync exists — no watch app required for this half.

**5.2 — Cue a workout on the Watch from our app (Runna-style: "start an incline treadmill walk").** This is the expensive half, and there are two real approaches with very different cost:

- **`WorkoutKit` (iOS 17.2+ / watchOS 10.2+)** lets an iPhone app *compose and schedule* a structured workout (warmup/work/cooldown blocks, target pace/incline/heart-rate-zone) and hand it to the Watch's own native Workout app to run — **without building a custom watchOS app at all**. This is Apple's own answer to "Runna-style workout push," and it gets you 80% of what you described (tap a button on the phone, the Watch prompts you to start a defined incline-treadmill workout) for a fraction of the cost of a full companion app, because there's no new target, no on-device Watch test loop, and no custom UI to build/maintain. The gap: you don't get custom on-wrist controls beyond what the native Workout app offers (e.g. you can't build a bespoke "bump incline +1%" button — you get what Apple's Workout app exposes for that activity type, which for treadmill is limited since the treadmill itself, not the Watch, controls incline on most machines anyway).
- **A full custom watchOS companion app** (`HKWorkoutSession` + `HKLiveWorkoutBuilder`, its own SwiftUI views on-wrist) is what you'd need if WorkoutKit's native Workout app UI doesn't cover what you actually want to control from the wrist. This is the Runna-equivalent build: new Xcode target, new entitlements, `WatchConnectivity` (or shared HealthKit/CloudKit state) to talk to the iPhone app, and a real on-device test loop, since the Watch Simulator does not reliably exercise `HKWorkoutSession` state transitions.

**Recommendation: try WorkoutKit first.** It's a small, scoped spike (a few days, not weeks) to see whether "start this defined incline-treadmill workout on my Watch" is achievable without a new target at all. Only fall back to a full companion watchOS app if that spike shows WorkoutKit's native workout UI is missing something you specifically need (which, given your setup is "start an incline treadmill walk" — a fairly standard structured cardio session — seems unlikely to require custom on-wrist UI). This ordering avoids paying for 2.4's expensive setup cost unless the API spike proves you actually need it.

## 7. Future phase: macro-balancing / "meal jigsaw" engine

Flagged as a later phase, not part of the punch list above — it's a genuinely different kind of feature (a solver, not a CRUD screen) and it has a hard dependency: it can't be built, or even meaningfully scoped, until Section 3's per-meal food logging exists, because it needs real per-food macro data to solve against. Worth writing down now while the shape of the idea is fresh, but this is a "come back to it" item.

**The problem, restated:** you're already doing this manually — pick breakfast, then mentally re-juggle lunch and dinner's protein/carb/fat to still land on the day's targets, and it's tedious enough that you want the app to do the arithmetic. Two directions this can go, worth separating because they're different amounts of engineering:

- **Direction A — "what's left, and how far off am I": a live remaining-budget view.** As you log meals through the day, show the remaining calorie/protein/carb/fat/fiber budget for the meals not yet logged, split evenly (or by your usual per-meal proportions) across however many slots are left. This is not a solver at all — it's a running subtraction against the targets you already have, using data the per-meal system already produces. Cheap, and honestly probably 80% of the value: "you've got 340 cal / 38g protein / 20g carbs / 15g fat left across lunch and dinner" already answers most of the "how do I balance the rest of today" question without any optimization.
- **Direction B — the actual jigsaw: "I want chia pudding and watermelon tomorrow, what do the other meals need to be?"** This is a real constraint-solving problem: fixed contributions from the meals you've already committed to, solve for what the remaining meal(s) need to contribute to land on target. Two ways to build it, in increasing order of ambition:
  - **Scale-what-you-already-eat:** for each remaining meal, you (or a "usual meals" list) supply a base food/recipe, and the engine solves the *quantity multiplier* on each remaining meal's foods that gets the day closest to target. This is a small linear system (one unknown per remaining meal, four-plus equations for calories/protein/carbs/fat/fiber) — solvable with basic linear algebra, not a "smart"/ML engine, and honestly more reliable than one, since the arithmetic is well-defined and small. The catch: a single scale factor moves all of a food's macros together, so it can get you close but can't independently fix "enough protein but too many carbs" within one food — real meals rarely land exactly on four+ targets simultaneously, and the UI needs to show the *closest achievable* result with its residual gap, not promise a perfect fit every time.
  - **Suggest a swap or addition from the catalog:** rather than scaling quantity, search `foods` for something that fills the specific remaining gap (e.g. "you're 30g short on protein and roughly on target for everything else — here's a food/quantity that adds ~30g protein without moving carbs/fat much"). This is closer to what "smart" suggests but is really just a search over the catalog scored by fit-to-residual-gap — a small optimization (minimize distance to the remaining target vector across a candidate list), not a model that needs training. Both of these are ordinary math (linear algebra / constrained search over a known catalog), not a case for an ML/LLM-based "engine" — the inputs are exact numbers with a small solution space, which is exactly where a classical solver beats a probabilistic one on both correctness and cost.

**Recommendation:** ship Direction A first (it requires nothing beyond what Section 3 already produces) and treat it as the visible "remaining budget" feature on the day view. Only pick up Direction B once Direction A is in daily use and you can tell whether the manual "still have to juggle it myself" friction Direction A leaves behind is actually worth solving with a real solver — it's the kind of feature that's easy to over-build before knowing if the simple version already covers most of the itch.

## 8. Near-term, independent feature: off-plan-day/weight correlation

Not gated behind anything above — the data this needs already exists today (`daily_checkins.yesterday_off_plan` + `body_weight_logs`/the existing EWMA trend calculator), so it can be picked up any time, including before the food-logging rebuild.

**What's actually happening, so the feature explains it correctly rather than alarming him:** a 1-3 day scale-weight bump after an off-plan meal is overwhelmingly water retention (sodium and extra glycogen each bind water), not fat gain — a single high-calorie day physically can't add that much fat mass that fast. The trend/EWMA line that already exists on the weight chart is the correct signal to trust; the raw daily number is the noisy one. So this feature isn't really "discover a hidden correlation" (the mechanism is well understood, not something to statistically fish for) — it's "surface the *known* explanation automatically instead of making Liam remember he logged off-plan two nights ago."

**Two pieces, worth building in this order:**

1. **Contextual annotation (cheap, high value).** On the weight chart / dashboard, when today's raw weight is up vs. the trend line *and* an off-plan flag was logged in the last 1-2 days, show an inline note referencing it directly (e.g. "Up 0.8kg vs. trend — you flagged an off-plan meal on Tue and Wed; this is typically water weight and settles in 2-3 days, your trend line hasn't moved"). This is a straightforward join between `daily_checkins` and `body_weight_logs` on date proximity — no statistics involved, just "was there a flag in the lookback window."
2. **Personal pattern stat (needs enough history to be meaningful — later).** Once there's enough off-plan-flagged history (realistically 15-20+ occurrences, or it's just noise), show something like "on average your weight is +Xkg on the day after an off-plan flag, based on N occurrences" computed from his own actual data. Worth gating this behind a minimum sample size and saying so in the UI (e.g. hide it, don't show a shaky number, until N is large enough) — a 3-data-point "average" would be actively misleading given how variable individual water retention is.

Both pieces are ordinary date-joined aggregate queries against data that already exists — no new schema, no ML, no dependency on the nutrition rebuild above.
